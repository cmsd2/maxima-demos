Digital Filter Design¶

This notebook designs a digital low-pass filter from first principles. We derive the analog Butterworth prototype symbolically, map it to a digital filter with the bilinear transform, and read off the coefficients of the difference equation. The filter then runs on a noisy signal, and an FFT checks the attenuation it achieves against the attenuation the design predicts.

A Chebyshev design at the end shows the trade between a flat passband and a steep transition.

The Butterworth Prototype [S]¶

An order-$n$ Butterworth low-pass filter has the flattest possible passband. Its squared magnitude response, with the cutoff normalised to $\omega_c = 1$, is

$$|H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \omega^{2n}}$$

Its poles sit evenly spaced on the unit circle in the left half of the $s$-plane:

$$s_k = e^{\,i\pi(2k + n - 1)/(2n)}, \quad k = 1, \dots, n$$

Multiplying out $\prod_k (s - s_k)$ gives the denominator polynomial. The complex poles come in conjugate pairs, so the coefficients are real.

In [2]:
butter_poles(n) := makelist(exp(%i*%pi*(2*k + n - 1)/(2*n)), k, 1, n)$
butter_den(n) := expand(realpart(rectform(
  expand(apply("*", map(lambda([p], s - p), butter_poles(n)))))))$

for n : 1 thru 3 do print("n =", n, ":  ", butter_den(n))$

n = 1 :   s+1
n = 2 :   s^2+sqrt(2)*s+1
n = 3 :   s^3+2*s^2+2*s+1

From $n = 4$ the exact coefficients involve $\cos(\pi/8)$ and friends, so we show them as decimals:

Frequency Response and Order [S+N]¶

In decibels the magnitude is $-10\log_{10}(1 + \omega^{2n})$. Every order passes through $-3$ dB at the cutoff, and above it the response falls by $20n$ dB per decade. A higher order buys a sharper cut-off at the cost of more poles to implement.

The Bilinear Transform [S]¶

A digital filter running at sample rate $f_s$ needs a transfer function in $z$. The bilinear transform substitutes

$$s = 2 f_s \, \frac{z - 1}{z + 1}$$

which maps the whole $j\omega$ axis onto the unit circle, so a stable analog filter gives a stable digital one. The mapping compresses frequency, so we pre-warp the cutoff: to place the digital cutoff at $f_c$, the analog prototype uses

$$\Omega_c = 2 f_s \tan\!\left(\frac{\pi f_c}{f_s}\right) = 2 f_s K$$

Scaling the prototype by $\Omega_c$ and substituting gives $H(z)$. The sample rate cancels, and the result depends only on $K$.

In [6]:
Ha : 1 / subst(s = s/(2*fs*K), butter_den(2))$
Hz : ratsimp(subst(s = 2*fs*(z - 1)/(z + 1), Ha));
Out[6]:

Dividing through by the leading coefficient of the denominator puts the filter in the standard form

$$H(z) = \frac{b_0 + b_1 z^{-1} + b_2 z^{-2}}{1 + a_1 z^{-1} + a_2 z^{-2}}$$

A 20 Hz Low-Pass at 1 kHz [S+N]¶

For $f_s = 1000$ Hz and $f_c = 20$ Hz we substitute $K = \tan(\pi f_c / f_s)$. The numerator coefficients are tiny because the cutoff is low compared with the sample rate.

In [8]:
fs_v : 1000$
fc_v : 20$
K_v : float(tan(%pi*fc_v/fs_v))$
b : float(subst(K = K_v, b_sym))$
a : float(subst(K = K_v, a_sym))$
print("b =", b)$
print("a =", a)$

b = [0.0036216815149286417,0.007243363029857283,0.0036216815149286417]
a = [1.0,-1.8226949251963083,0.8371816512560226]

The digital response follows from evaluating $H(z)$ on the unit circle, $z = e^{2\pi i f / f_s}$. Writing $z^{-k} = \cos k\theta - i \sin k\theta$ with $\theta = 2\pi f / f_s$ keeps the arithmetic real, and gives the gain and phase directly. The response matches the analog prototype at low frequencies, crosses $-3$ dB at exactly 20 Hz thanks to the pre-warping, and falls to zero at the Nyquist frequency of 500 Hz, where the bilinear transform maps $\omega = \infty$.

In [9]:
/* [gain, phase] of H(z) at z = exp(i*theta), theta = 2*pi*f/fs */
freq_resp(f) := block([th : float(2*%pi*f/fs_v), nr, ni, dr, di],
  nr : b[1] + b[2]*cos(th) + b[3]*cos(2*th),
  ni : -(b[2]*sin(th) + b[3]*sin(2*th)),
  dr : 1 + a[2]*cos(th) + a[3]*cos(2*th),
  di : -(a[2]*sin(th) + a[3]*sin(2*th)),
  [sqrt(nr^2 + ni^2) / sqrt(dr^2 + di^2), atan2(ni, nr) - atan2(di, dr)])$
gain_db(f) := 20*log(first(freq_resp(f)))/log(10.0)$

f_list : np_to_list(np_logspace(0, log(499.0)/log(10.0), 300))$
digital_db : map(gain_db, f_list)$
analog_db : map(lambda([f], float(-10*log(1 + (f/fc_v)^4)/log(10.0))), f_list)$

print("Gain at 20 Hz:", gain_db(20), "dB")$

ax_draw2d(
  color="steelblue", line_width=2, name="Digital (bilinear)", lines(f_list, digital_db),
  color="darkorange", dash="dash", name="Analog prototype", lines(f_list, analog_db),

Poles and Zeros [S+N]¶

The bilinear transform sends the analog zeros at infinity to $z = -1$, so this filter has a double zero there. Its two poles are a conjugate pair just inside the unit circle. They sit close to $z = 1$ because the cutoff is low, and a pole that close to the circle means a slowly decaying impulse response.

Filtering a Noisy Signal [N]¶

The test signal is a 5 Hz sine we want to keep, a 120 Hz interference tone we want to remove, and white noise. The filter runs the difference equation

$$y_n = b_0 x_n + b_1 x_{n-1} + b_2 x_{n-2} - a_1 y_{n-1} - a_2 y_{n-2}$$

sample by sample, as it would on a microcontroller.

In [11]:
np_seed(42)$
n_s : 1000$
t_nd : np_scale(1.0/fs_v, np_arange(n_s))$
clean : np_sin(np_scale(2*%pi*5.0, t_nd))$
hum : np_scale(0.5, np_sin(np_scale(2*%pi*120.0, t_nd)))$
x_nd : np_add(np_add(clean, hum), np_scale(0.2, np_randn([n_s])))$

iir_filter(b, a, xs) := block([ys : [], x1 : 0.0, x2 : 0.0, y1 : 0.0, y2 : 0.0, yn],
  for xn in xs do (
    yn : b[1]*xn + b[2]*x1 + b[3]*x2 - a[2]*y1 - a[3]*y2,
    ys : cons(yn, ys),
    x2 : x1, x1 : xn, y2 : y1, y1 : yn),
  reverse(ys))$

t_list : np_to_list(t_nd)$
y_list : iir_filter(b, a, np_to_list(x_nd))$

ax_draw2d(
  color="lightgray", name="Input", lines(t_list, np_to_list(x_nd)),
  color="darkorange", dash="dash", name="5 Hz component", lines(t_list, np_to_list(clean)),
  color="steelblue", line_width=2, name="Filtered", lines(t_list, y_list),

Checking the Attenuation with an FFT [S+N]¶

The design predicts how much the filter suppresses the 120 Hz tone. The FFTs of the input and output measure it. With a 1 s record each FFT bin is 1 Hz wide, so bin 120 holds the interference. We skip the first 200 ms of output, where the start-up transient sits, and compare spectra over the remaining 0.8 s.

Butterworth versus Chebyshev [S+N]¶

A Chebyshev type I filter allows ripple in the passband in exchange for a steeper transition. Its squared magnitude uses the Chebyshev polynomial $T_n$:

$$|H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \varepsilon^2 T_n^2(\omega)}$$

With $\varepsilon = \sqrt{10^{r/10} - 1}$ the passband ripples by $r$ dB. For $n = 4$:

In [14]:
T4 : expand(chebyshev_t(4, w));
ripple_db : 1.0$
eps2 : 10^(ripple_db/10) - 1$
cheb_db(w) := float(-10*log(1 + eps2*chebyshev_t(4, w)^2)/log(10.0))$
butt_db(w) := float(-10*log(1 + w^8)/log(10.0))$

print("At 2 x cutoff: Butterworth", butt_db(2.0), "dB, Chebyshev", cheb_db(2.0), "dB")$

ax_draw2d(
  color="steelblue", line_width=2, name="Butterworth n = 4",
  lines(w_list, map(butt_db, w_list)),
  color="crimson", line_width=2, name="Chebyshev n = 4, 1 dB ripple",

At twice the cutoff the Chebyshev filter is far deeper into its stopband than a Butterworth filter of the same order, but its passband wobbles by the 1 dB we allowed.

Summary¶

Step Method
Analog prototype Symbolic: poles on the unit circle, expanded denominator
Digital mapping Symbolic: bilinear transform with pre-warping, coefficients in terms of $K$
Response and poles Numeric: evaluation on the unit circle, allroots
Filtering Numeric: the difference equation, sample by sample
Verification np_fft: measured attenuation at 120 Hz against the design

The symbolic derivation gives coefficient formulas that hold for any sample rate and cutoff, and the numeric half confirms the filter does what the formulas promise.
