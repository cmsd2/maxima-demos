Robot Arm Kinematics¶

This notebook works through the kinematics of a two-link planar robot arm, the geometry behind every pick-and-place robot. We derive the forward kinematics and the Jacobian symbolically, find the configurations where the arm loses a direction of motion, and solve the inverse problem in closed form. The closed-form solution moves the arm along a path.

A numerical Newton solver then reaches the same answers iteratively, as a controller would for an arm too complex for a closed form, and a final section shows why joint speeds blow up as the arm approaches a singular configuration.

Forward Kinematics [S]¶

Each joint rotates the rest of the arm and each link translates along its own axis. In homogeneous coordinates a rotation by $\theta$ followed by a translation $l$ along the rotated $x$ axis is the matrix

$$A(\theta, l) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & l\cos\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & l\sin\theta \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

The end effector's position is the last column of $A(\theta_1, l_1)\,A(\theta_2, l_2)$.

The result is the familiar pair $x = l_1\cos\theta_1 + l_2\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$, $y = l_1\sin\theta_1 + l_2\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2)$. The elbow joint sits at the end of the first link.

The Jacobian and Singularities [S]¶

The Jacobian $J = \partial(x, y)/\partial(\theta_1, \theta_2)$ maps joint velocities to end-effector velocity, $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = J\dot{\boldsymbol\theta}$. Where its determinant vanishes the arm cannot move in some direction, however fast its joints turn.

In [4]:
J : jacobian(fk, [theta1, theta2])$
detJ : trigsimp(trigexpand(determinant(J)))$
[J, 'determinant('J) = detJ];
Out[4]:

The determinant $l_1 l_2 \sin\theta_2$ vanishes at $\theta_2 = 0$, where the arm is fully stretched, and at $\theta_2 = \pi$, where it is folded back on itself. Neither depends on $\theta_1$. Both lie on the edge of the workspace, so the boundary of what the arm can reach is exactly where it loses mobility.

Inverse Kinematics in Closed Form [S]¶

Given a target $(x, y)$, the law of cosines in the triangle formed by the two links and the line from the base to the target gives the elbow angle:

$$\cos\theta_2 = \frac{x^2 + y^2 - l_1^2 - l_2^2}{2 l_1 l_2}$$

Both signs of $\theta_2$ work. For a target above the $x$ axis, positive $\theta_2$ puts the elbow below the line from the base to the target (elbow down) and negative $\theta_2$ puts it above (elbow up). The shoulder angle is the direction to the target less the angle the forearm adds:

$$\theta_1 = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x) - \operatorname{atan2}(l_2\sin\theta_2,\; l_1 + l_2\cos\theta_2)$$

Inverse Kinematics by Newton's Method [N]¶

Most arms have no closed-form inverse, and controllers solve $f(\boldsymbol\theta) = \mathbf{x}_{\text{target}}$ numerically. Newton's method linearises with the Jacobian at each step:

$$\boldsymbol\theta_{k+1} = \boldsymbol\theta_k + J(\boldsymbol\theta_k)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{x}_{\text{target}} - f(\boldsymbol\theta_k)\right)$$

Starting from a rough guess, the error falls quadratically: each step roughly squares it.

In [10]:
J_num : subst(links, J)$
J_f : buildq([e : J_num], lambda([t1, t2], float(subst([theta1 = t1, theta2 = t2], e))))$

newton_ik(target, q0, steps) := block([q : q0, err],
  for k : 1 thru steps do (
    err : target - fk_f(q[1], q[2]),
    printf(true, "step ~d: error ~,3e~%", k - 1, sqrt(err[1]^2 + err[2]^2)),
    q : q + np_to_list(np_flatten(np_solve(ndarray(J_f(q[1], q[2])),
                                           ndarray(transpose(matrix(err))))))),
  q)$

Newton's method converges to the solution with positive $\theta_2$ because the starting guess has a positive elbow angle. A guess with a negative elbow angle finds the other solution.

Approaching a Singularity [S+N]¶

Suppose the tip moves straight out along the $x$ axis at unit speed towards full reach. The joint velocities needed are $\dot{\boldsymbol\theta} = J^{-1}\dot{\mathbf{x}}$, and $J^{-1}$ contains $1/\det J = 1/(l_1 l_2 \sin\theta_2)$. As the arm straightens, $\sin\theta_2 \to 0$ and the joints must spin ever faster.

The manipulability $w = |\det J|$ measures how far a configuration is from a singular one.
