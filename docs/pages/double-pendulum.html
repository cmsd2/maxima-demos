The Double Pendulum: Lagrangian Mechanics and Chaos¶

A double pendulum hangs one pendulum from the bob of another. Its equations of motion take a page to derive by hand from forces, because the rod tensions couple the two bobs. Lagrange's method avoids the tensions altogether: we write the kinetic and potential energy in the two angles, and Maxima differentiates its way to the equations of motion.

Near the hanging position the system has two normal modes whose frequencies come out in closed form. At large amplitude the motion becomes chaotic: CVODE integrates it while conserving energy to many digits, and two runs that start $10^{-9}$ rad apart end up on unrelated paths within seconds. The growth rate of that separation gives the largest Lyapunov exponent.

The Lagrangian [S]¶

Two point masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ hang on massless rods of lengths $l_1$ and $l_2$. The angles $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ are measured from the downward vertical, so the bob positions are

$$x_1 = l_1\sin\theta_1,\quad y_1 = -l_1\cos\theta_1,\qquad x_2 = x_1 + l_2\sin\theta_2,\quad y_2 = y_1 - l_2\cos\theta_2$$

Declaring that both angles depend on $t$ lets diff apply the chain rule to the positions. The Lagrangian is $L = T - V$, with $T$ the kinetic energy of the two bobs and $V = m_1 g y_1 + m_2 g y_2$.

depends([theta1, theta2], t)$

x2 : x1 + l2*sin(theta2)$
y2 : y1 - l2*cos(theta2)$

T : trigreduce(trigsimp(expand(m1/2*(diff(x1, t)^2 + diff(y1, t)^2)
                              + m2/2*(diff(x2, t)^2 + diff(y2, t)^2))))$
V : expand(m1*g*y1 + m2*g*y2)$
L : T - V;

Euler–Lagrange Equations [S]¶

Each angle obeys $\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta_i} - \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_i} = 0$. Maxima differentiates with respect to $\dot\theta_i$ directly. Both equations are linear in the angular accelerations, so we replace $\ddot\theta_1, \ddot\theta_2$ by symbols $a_1, a_2$ and read off the coefficient matrix. That matrix is the configuration-dependent mass matrix $M(\theta)$.

In [3]:
EL(q) := diff(diff(L, diff(q, t)), t) - diff(L, q)$
eqs : trigsimp(subst([diff(theta1, t, 2) = a1, diff(theta2, t, 2) = a2],
                     [EL(theta1), EL(theta2)]))$
M_theta : trigreduce(coefmatrix(eqs, [a1, a2]));
Out[3]:

The off-diagonal term $m_2 l_1 l_2\cos(\theta_2 - \theta_1)$ couples the two angles. Solving the pair of linear equations gives the two angular accelerations as explicit functions of the angles and angular velocities.

Small Oscillations [S]¶

Near the hanging equilibrium $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0$ the equations linearise to $M_0\,\ddot{\boldsymbol\theta} + K\boldsymbol\theta = 0$. The mass matrix $M_0$ is $M(\theta)$ at the equilibrium, and the stiffness matrix $K$ is the Hessian of the potential energy there. The quadratic velocity terms drop out because they are second order.

In [5]:
at_rest : [theta1 = 0, theta2 = 0]$
M0 : subst(at_rest, M_theta)$
K : subst(at_rest, hessian(V, [theta1, theta2]))$
['M[0] = M0, 'K = K];
Out[5]:

A solution $\boldsymbol\theta = \mathbf{v}\cos\omega t$ requires $\det(K - \omega^2 M_0) = 0$, a quadratic in $\omega^2$. For equal masses $m$ and equal lengths $l$ its roots are $\omega^2 = (g/l)(2 \mp \sqrt2)$. The first row of $(K - \omega^2 M_0)\mathbf{v} = 0$ fixes each mode shape $\mathbf{v} = (1, r)$.

In [6]:
equal_ml : [m1 = m, m2 = m, l1 = l, l2 = l]$
M0e : subst(equal_ml, M0)$
Ke : subst(equal_ml, K)$
/* write omega^2 = s g/l and solve for the dimensionless s */
s_roots : map(rhs, solve(determinant(Ke - s*(g/l)*M0e), s))$
s_roots : sort(s_roots, lambda([a, b], is(float(a) < float(b))))$
w2_roots : s_roots*g/l$
shape(sv) := block([algebraic : true],
  ratsimp(-(Ke[1, 1] - sv*(g/l)*M0e[1, 1])/(Ke[1, 2] - sv*(g/l)*M0e[1, 2])))$
[omega[1]^2 = w2_roots[1], r[1] = shape(s_roots[1]),
 omega[2]^2 = w2_roots[2], r[2] = shape(s_roots[2])];
Out[6]:

The slow mode swings both bobs the same way, with the lower bob's angle $\sqrt2$ times the upper one's. The fast mode swings them in opposite directions.

Checking the Modes Numerically [N]¶

From here on $m = 1$ kg, $l = 1$ m and $g = 9.81$ m/s². The eigenvalues of $M_0^{-1}K$ are the squared frequencies, and its eigenvectors are the mode shapes. np_eig computes both from the numeric matrices.

In [7]:
params : [m = 1, l = 1, g = 9.81]$
Mn : ndarray(float(subst(params, M0e)))$
Kn : ndarray(float(subst(params, Ke)))$
[evals, evecs] : np_eig(np_matmul(np_inv(Mn), Kn))$
evals : np_to_list(evals)$
evecs : np_to_matrix(evecs)$
order : sort([1, 2], lambda([i, j], evals[i] < evals[j]))$
for k : 1 thru 2 do block([i : order[k], exact_w2, exact_r],
  exact_w2 : float(subst(params, w2_roots[k])),
  exact_r : float(shape(s_roots[k])),
  printf(true, "mode ~d: omega^2 = ~,10f (exact ~,10f, diff ~,1e), r = ~,10f (exact ~,10f)~%",
         k, evals[i], exact_w2, abs(evals[i] - exact_w2),
         evecs[2, i]/evecs[1, i], exact_r))$

The Modes in Motion [S+N]¶

The full nonlinear equations are the right-hand side for CVODE, with state $(\theta_1, \theta_2, \omega_1, \omega_2)$. Starting at rest in a mode shape with amplitude 0.001 rad, the pendulum should stay in that shape and oscillate at the mode frequency. CVODE's event detection records each upward zero crossing of $\theta_1$, and the spacing of those crossings measures the period.

In [8]:
derivs : [diff(theta1, t) = w1, diff(theta2, t) = w2]$
rhs_num : float(subst(params, subst(equal_ml, subst(derivs, [w1, w2, acc[1], acc[2]]))))$
state : [t, theta1, theta2, w1, w2]$
t_modes : np_linspace(0.0, 8.0, 801)$

mode_runs : makelist(block([r_k, sol, crossings, period, exact],
    r_k : float(shape(s_roots[k])),
    sol : np_cvode(rhs_num, state, [0.001, 0.001*r_k, 0.0, 0.0], t_modes,
                   1e-12, 1e-12, adams, [theta1], 5000, [1]),
    crossings : map(first, second(sol)),
    period : (last(crossings) - first(crossings))/(length(crossings) - 1),
    exact : float(2*%pi/sqrt(subst(params, w2_roots[k]))),
    printf(true, "mode ~d: period ~,6f s (linear theory ~,6f s, relative diff ~,1e)~%",
           k, period, exact, abs(period/exact - 1)),
    first(sol)), k, 1, 2)$

mode 1: period 2.621053 s (linear theory 2.621052 s, relative diff 7.5e-8)
mode 2: period 1.085677 s (linear theory 1.085675 s, relative diff 1.9e-6)

The measured periods differ from linear theory by parts in $10^{7}$ and $10^{6}$. A run at 0.01 rad gives differences 100 times larger, so they scale as the amplitude squared and come from the nonlinear terms, not from the integration. The plots show both angles for each mode: in the slow mode they rise and fall together, and in the fast mode they move in opposition.

Energy Conservation [S+N]¶

No friction acts, so $E = T + V$ must stay at its initial value. The symbolic energy, with the numeric parameters substituted, becomes a function of the four state variables. Its relative drift over the run measures the integration error independently of the equations of motion.

In [11]:
E_expr : float(subst(params, subst(equal_ml, subst(derivs, T + V))))$
E_f : buildq([e : E_expr], lambda([theta1, theta2, w1, w2], e))$
E_t : map(lambda([r], E_f(r[2], r[3], r[4], r[5])), rows)$
E0 : first(E_t)$
drift : lmax(map(lambda([e], abs(e - E0)), E_t))/abs(E0)$
printf(true, "E(0) = ~,10f J, max relative drift over 20 s: ~,2e~%", E0, drift)$

Sensitivity to Initial Conditions [N]¶

A second run starts with $\theta_1$ larger by $10^{-9}$ rad. The distance between the two runs in the four-dimensional phase space, $d(t) = |\mathbf{y}_A(t) - \mathbf{y}_B(t)|$, grows roughly as $e^{\lambda t}$ while it is small, where $\lambda$ is the largest Lyapunov exponent. For contrast, the same perturbation goes into a low-energy start at $\theta_1 = \theta_2 = 0.3$ rad, close to the linear regime.

The high-energy separation climbs nine orders of magnitude in about ten seconds and then saturates at the size of the phase space. The low-energy separation stays below $10^{-8}$ for the whole run.

Estimating the Lyapunov Exponent [N]¶

A least-squares line through $\ln d(t)$ over the growth phase gives $\lambda$. The window starts where $d$ first exceeds $10^{-8}$, after the initial transient, and ends where it first exceeds $10^{-2}$, before saturation. The same fit over the whole low-energy run gives a slope near zero.

In [13]:
first_above(d, level) := block([i : 1], while d[i] <= level do i : i + 1, i)$
slope(ts, ys) := block([n : length(ts), tm, ym],
  tm : apply("+", ts)/n, ym : apply("+", ys)/n,
  apply("+", (ts - tm)*(ys - ym))/apply("+", (ts - tm)^2))$

i0 : first_above(d_high, 1e-8)$
i1 : first_above(d_high, 1e-2)$
lambda_high : slope(rest(firstn(times, i1), i0 - 1), map(log, rest(firstn(d_high, i1), i0 - 1)))$
lambda_low : slope(times, map(log, d_low))$
printf(true, "growth window: t = ~,2f s to ~,2f s~%", times[i0], times[i1])$
printf(true, "high energy: lambda = ~,2f 1/s, separation e-folds every ~,2f s~%",
       lambda_high, 1/lambda_high)$
printf(true, "low energy:  slope = ~,4f 1/s, largest separation ~,1e~%",
       lambda_low, lmax(d_low))$

growth window: t = 0.82 s to 8.52 s
high energy: lambda = 1.82 1/s, separation e-folds every 0.55 s
low energy:  slope = 0.0086 1/s, largest separation 6.7e-9

A single pair of runs gives a finite-time estimate that varies with the window and the starting point. Averaging over many starts on the same energy surface would tighten it. The low-energy motion is quasi-periodic: the perturbation shifts the phase of the two modes slightly and the separation grows no faster than linearly.
